Privacy policy · 6 October 2026

Your network stays yours

Perimeter does not collect anything about you. What it learns about your home network stays on your Mac, and the latest answer stays on your iPhone.

In short

  • No account, no sign-in, no analytics, no adverts and no tracking.
  • Nothing about your network or your devices is sent to us, or to anyone else.
  • The one optional download is a public list of hardware makers, and only when you ask for it.
  • You can delete everything yourself. Here is how.

Who this is from

Perimeter is made by David Cvetkovski. “We” in this policy means him. It covers Perimeter for Mac, Perimeter for iPhone and this website.

Because nothing about you is sent to us, we have nothing of yours to look up, correct or delete. Everything Perimeter knows is on your own devices, under your control.

What Perimeter looks at

Only the networks you have told it to watch, and only on your Mac. Each time it checks, it finds out which devices are on your network, and what they call themselves. It also asks your router what doors it has open to the internet and whether push-button pairing (WPS) is on, reads how your Mac’s Wi-Fi is protected, and writes down when your Mac was asleep.

About once a day it gives each device on your own network a single knock on a few risky doors: a connection opened and closed at once. Nothing is sent through it, and no password is ever tried.

It looks at nothing on any other network, and at nothing out on the internet.

What your Mac keeps

Everything is kept on your Mac. Most of it is in a folder in your home folder, a hidden one, because its name starts with a dot. It is not copied anywhere by Perimeter. If you back up your Mac, your backup includes it, as it includes the rest of your home folder.

What it saw
Which devices were on your network and when: their names, makers, addresses on your network and hardware addresses (MAC). The hardware addresses never leave your Mac, not even for your iPhone.
What you told it
Which network you chose to watch, the names you gave devices, and what you answered: “It’s mine”, “I don’t know it”, “Got it”.
What it worked out
The notes in your checkup, and the times your Mac was asleep.
Phone access
Only if you paired an iPhone: a secret code and a certificate that let that iPhone, and only it, read your Mac. Turning off phone access deletes them.
Settings and records
Its settings, a record of what Perimeter itself did, a couple of small settings of the Mac app, and, if you asked for it, the list of hardware makers.
Start-up files
Two small files in your Library folder that start the checks, and the dashboard in your web browser, when you log in.

What your iPhone keeps

The pairing
Your Mac’s name and address on your home network, and the secret code and certificate that go with them. They are kept in the iPhone’s Keychain, on this iPhone only: they are not synced to iCloud and not in backups.
The last answer
What your Mac last said: the checkup, and the names, makers and addresses of your devices, never hardware addresses. The app and its widget show it until your Mac answers again. It is kept out of backups too. On the lock screen, the widget hides names until you unlock your iPhone.
A few settings
Whether a Mac is paired, whether it has asked about notifications, and how much of your Mac’s news it has shown.
The sample home
Nothing. It is made up, and nothing in it is kept.

The app asks to use your local network, to reach your Mac, and nothing else. It does not use your camera (to pair, you point your iPhone’s own Camera app at the code on your Mac), your photos, contacts, microphone or location. It notices whether your iPhone is on Wi-Fi, so that it can tell you when you are away from home, but it does not read the name of the Wi-Fi.

Your iPhone talks to your Mac, and to nothing else. The connection is encrypted, and the iPhone refuses any Mac but the one it was paired with. When you are away from home it simply cannot reach your Mac, and says so; nothing is routed through the internet or through any server of ours. Now and then, when iOS allows it, the app asks your Mac for news in the background, so that the widget and notifications stay up to date.

Notifications

The Mac app and the iPhone app each ask before they send any. They tell you when something needs a look, such as a new device. They are made on your own device and never go through us or through any push service: your Mac makes its own, and your iPhone makes its own when it hears from your Mac. A notification can name a device, so your iPhone’s own settings decide whether it shows on a locked screen. You can turn them off at any time in Settings.

What leaves your Mac

Nothing about you, your network or your devices is sent to us. Three things do leave your Mac, and nothing else does:

  • Questions about names. To find the names your router gave your devices, Perimeter asks your Mac’s usual name lookup about the addresses on your own network. At home that is your router. If you have set your Mac to use a different one, those questions go there. They hold only the private addresses of your own devices, never names, makers or hardware addresses.
  • The list of hardware makers, when you ask. Perimeter downloads a public list from the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), about 4 MB, so that devices can show who made them. It is a request for a public file, like opening a web page: the IEEE’s server can see your internet address, and that it was Perimeter, and which version, that asked. Nothing about your network is sent with it, and the lookup itself happens on your Mac.
  • Checking for updates, when you choose it. The Mac app reads one small file from this website to see whether there is a newer version. Nothing is sent but the request any browser sends for a page. Nothing is checked, or downloaded, by itself.

This website

This website is a few plain pages. It has no cookies, no analytics, no adverts and no scripts. It is hosted by GitHub Pages, which, like any web host, may keep ordinary records of visits, such as your internet address and the page you asked for. We do not see them.

Apple, and other people’s code

When you get Perimeter for iPhone from the App Store, Apple deals with you as it always does, under its own policy. If you have agreed to share analytics with app developers in your iPhone’s settings, Apple may give us totals and crash reports with no names in them. Perimeter does not add anything to them.

Perimeter for Mac carries a copy of Python, a widely used programming language, so that it can run its checks. It runs on your Mac and contacts no one. Perimeter for iPhone uses no code from anyone but Apple.

Deleting everything

Everything is on your own devices, so you can remove it all yourself.

On your iPhone

  1. Open Perimeter, open its settings and choose “Unpair this iPhone”. That removes the pairing and the last answer.
  2. Delete the app, as you would any app.

To unpair every iPhone at once, choose “Pair iPhone” in the Mac’s menu bar panel, then “Turn off phone access”.

On your Mac

  1. In Perimeter’s panel in the menu bar, choose “Pair iPhone”, then “Turn off phone access”, then choose Quit.
  2. In Finder, choose Go, then “Go to Folder…”, type ~/Library/LaunchAgents and press Return. Drag the two files whose names start with dev.perimeter to the Bin.
  3. Restart your Mac, so that the checks stop.
  4. In Finder, choose Go, then “Go to Folder…”, type ~/.perimeter and press Return. Drag the folder it opens, with everything Perimeter learned, to the Bin. If you cannot find it, go one step up to your home folder and press Command, Shift and full stop to show hidden folders.
  5. Drag Perimeter from your Applications folder to the Bin, then empty the Bin.

The Mac app also leaves a tiny settings file about itself in your Library folder. It holds nothing about your network.

Changes, and how to reach us
